PROJECT / PREDICTIVE MAINTENANCE

Predictive Maintenance

Exploring the move from reactive repairs to data-informed maintenance.

The business problem

How can maintenance move from reacting to machine failures to anticipating them? An unexpected breakdown can interrupt production, delay orders, and leave maintenance teams responding under pressure.

I explored how sensor data and machine learning could support maintenance decisions. The challenge was to detect failures while limiting false alarms: frequent unnecessary warnings can waste time and make people less likely to trust the system.

The dataset

I used the AI4I 2020 Predictive Maintenance dataset, a public synthetic dataset containing 10,000 records designed to reflect industrial maintenance conditions.

Its measurements include air and process temperatures, rotational speed, torque, and tool wear, alongside product type and failure labels. Only around 3.4% of records represent failures, making this an imbalanced classification problem.

That imbalance matters. A model could appear highly accurate simply by predicting normal operation most of the time, while missing the failures it was meant to identify.

Preparation and feature engineering

I explored the sensor measurements and failure distribution, then developed features that represented relationships between the measurements.

  • Power: Combined torque and rotational speed to represent machine workload.
  • Overstrain: Combined tool wear and torque to represent accumulated mechanical stress.
  • Temperature difference: Captured the difference between process and air temperatures.

The aim was to give the model useful representations of operating conditions, alongside the original measurements.

From baseline to final model

I started with Logistic Regression to establish a baseline. It detected many failures, but generated substantially more false alarms than correct failure alerts.

I then developed a Random Forest model to capture more complex relationships between the inputs. I examined feature importance to understand which measurements contributed most to its predictions; torque, engineered power, and rotational speed ranked prominently.

The improvement came with a trade-off. Precision increased sharply, meaning a much larger share of failure alerts were correct, while recall decreased slightly, meaning the model missed a somewhat larger share of actual failures.

For a maintenance team, both measures matter. The preferred balance depends on the cost of investigating an unnecessary alert compared with the consequences of missing a failure.

Making the results usable

I built a Streamlit demonstration where users could adjust sensor inputs and inspect the model’s response. It brought the modelling work into an interface for exploring operating scenarios.

The presentation also describes a multi-output classifier for suggesting failure categories. These suggestions support investigation; they do not establish a confirmed physical root cause.

Limitations

  • Synthetic data: Performance on this dataset does not establish performance on real production equipment.
  • Prediction timing: The classification results do not demonstrate how far in advance a failure could be predicted.
  • Evaluation verification: Reported metrics still need reproduction from code, including checks of the data split, preprocessing, and exclusion of failure-label information from predictive inputs.
  • Remaining missed failures: The final model’s reported recall of 82% means some failures were still missed.
  • Business impact: Reduced downtime, maintenance costs, and financial savings were potential benefits, not measured outcomes of this capstone.

Business value and next steps

This project helped me connect model evaluation with a practical business decision: when is an alert useful enough to act on?

A real deployment would need equipment-specific validation, evidence of sufficient warning time, and feedback from maintenance specialists. A sensible next step would be a monitored pilot that records correct alerts, missed failures, unnecessary inspections, and maintenance outcomes before connecting predictions to work-order systems.
